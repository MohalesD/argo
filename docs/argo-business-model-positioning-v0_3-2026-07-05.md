## 1. The Argonauts Narrative

Every company that's actually good at hiring has a version of this
story, whether they tell it out loud or not: at some point they got the
right five people in a room, the ones who actually fit, motivation and
skill and culture all pointing the same direction, and something shifted.
Everyone else on the team could feel it. That's not a resume match.
That's a crew.

The Argonauts were Jason's crew, hand-picked, each one chosen for a
reason, sailing after something worth the risk. Argo was the ship that
got them there. That's the whole idea, and it's not subtle on purpose:
Argo helps a company build its own crew of Argonauts, the specific
people who fit this specific mission, not just whoever cleared the
resume screen. The Golden Fleece is what you're actually after. Not "a
hire." The hire that, a year later, everyone still points to as the one
that changed the trajectory of the team.

This has to stay a confident story, not a cute one. Nobody wants HR
software with a mascot. The myth gives Argo a name and a reason for the
gold, it doesn't give it a personality that undercuts the fact that this
is serious software for people who take hiring seriously.

## 2. The BHAG and the Radical Message

Pulled forward from Mo's own notes, because the ambition was already
written down years ago and it sets the ceiling for everything else.

**The BHAG (Big Hairy Audacious Goal):** to be the Salesforce or
Microsoft of HR and People Tech, but genuinely a pleasure to use. Stated
elsewhere in the notes as "the operating system of People Ops." That's
the long-range destination. Argo starts at the interview layer because
that's the wedge, not because that's the ambition.

**The radical message.** The best category-defining companies each
picked a fight with the old way and said it in three words. Salesforce:
"No Software." Drift: "No Forms." Slack, in spirit: kill the internal
email. The notes ask, correctly, what Argo's version is. The working
candidate straight out of the notes is "unbundling from the ATS,"
the idea that interviewing is too important to be a neglected tab inside
a bloated applicant tracking system (ATS), and it deserves its own tool
built by people who actually care about it.

**[DECISION NEEDED]** The radical message isn't locked. "Unbundling from
the ATS" is a strong, honest, category-aware candidate, and it's got real
lineage in your own thinking, but it's insider language, a hiring manager
might not feel it the way a People Ops leader would. Worth workshopping a
version that lands for the actual buyer before it goes on a homepage.
Naming this as an open decision rather than quietly picking one.

**Why the ATS incumbents don't crush this, in your own framing.** The old
notes make the competitive argument cleanly: legacy ATS and HRIS (human
resources information system) products stay slow because of dated
architecture, dated brand, and all-or-nothing pricing that bundles dozens
of mediocre features. They do many things adequately. Argo does one thing
extremely well. That's the moat's first layer, and it only holds if Argo
actually stays disciplined about doing one thing extremely well, which is
a product-scope commitment, not just a marketing line.

## 3. Position Statement

Rewritten from the neutral version in the discovery and persona master,
now free to actually use the name.

For job interviewers who need an easy, repeatable way to ask and
document interview questions and responses, Argo is a web-based platform
that gives quick access to role- and level-specific interview questions,
pulled from a verified bank, while letting the interviewer log responses
and assessments in real time. Unlike printouts, spreadsheets, or an open
tab to whatever AI tool is closest, Argo gives interviewers a systematic
way to reduce bias, run a consistent process, compare assessments across
candidates and fellow interviewers, and store everything centrally.
Argo exists to help a company build its own crew of Argonauts, the
specific people who actually fit this specific mission, not just
whoever cleared the resume screen.

**[DECISION NEEDED]** That last sentence is the Argonauts framing doing
real work in the position statement itself, not just sitting in a
narrative section above it. Worth deciding if that's too much this
early, some companies want the myth in marketing copy but not in the
literal product position statement, versus it being exactly the kind of
thing that makes Argo sound like something instead of another ATS
(applicant tracking system) feature.

## 4. Visual Identity Direction

Full brand guidelines are in progress through Claude Design, and logo
concepts for the sheep and ship motifs are running through a separate
image tool. Here's the direction already locked in, so nothing else
built in the meantime contradicts it:

1. Gold and amber as the primary palette, referencing the Golden Fleece.
   Bright and immediately readable as gold to an ordinary viewer, not a
   dark bronze, ochre, or mustard tone, even where a technically "real"
   gold would trend darker. Popular perception matters more here than
   mineralogical accuracy.
2. Dark forest green in a minor supporting or accent role, for
   differentiation, readability, or emphasis in detailed spaces. Not
   banned, just not primary.
3. A generous amount of white and light cream as the base, not the gold
   itself carrying most of the surface area.
4. Ship and sheep motifs, explored across a style range from literal
   mascot-adjacent to fully abstract, landing roughly two-thirds toward
   the realistic end, recognizable as an actual sheep or ship, simplified
   enough to work as a small mark, not illustrated.
5. Body text stays a dark, readable warm gray, never pure black, never
   the gold itself.
6. **The feel, not just the palette.** Argo is B2B SaaS (business-to-
   business software as a service) by buyer and by function, HR and
   People Ops professionals evaluating candidates for a living, but it's
   a PLG (product-led growth) play, not a sales-led one, and the visual
   language should carry that. Most B2B SaaS in this category reads
   cold and forgettable on purpose, safe, beige, interchangeable. Argo
   should read closer to something a strong consumer brand would have
   built: open, a little luxurious to actually use, the kind of thing
   worth someone showing a colleague unprompted, while still landing as
   sophisticated enough that a buyer takes it seriously. Not consumer
   because the audience is consumers. Consumer in craft, because that's
   what makes a B2B tool actually spread inside a company instead of
   sitting there being tolerated.

## 5. Business Model Canvas, 2026 Working Draft

Rebuilt from the structure of the 2019 Lean Canvas and the 2020 Argo Biz
Model Canvas (both in `Business_Model__Lean_Canvas_Pop_Interview__v1_
190925_MED.xlsx`). Where a cell below has a number in it, that number is
carried from the old canvas and is explicitly six years stale, marked so
it can't be mistaken for a current target. Where a cell is left as an
open question instead of a placeholder guess, that's on purpose, a
guessed number would be worse than an honest blank.

**Problem**
1. Not knowing what questions to ask for a given role and level.
2. No repeatable, objective structure across interviewers.
3. No central place to capture and compare responses and assessments.

**Existing Alternatives**
Googling questions, a shared cloud doc, gut feeling, an ATS with limited
functionality, an open tab to ChatGPT or Claude with no way to verify
the output. See Section 4 of the discovery and persona master for the
full 2026 breakdown of alternative tiers.

**Unique Value Proposition**
A verified, role- and level-specific question set pulled from a real
bank, not generated fresh and hoped for, plus real-time capture and a
consistent rating scale shared automatically across the team.

**Unfair Advantage**
The 2019 canvas left this blank, and filling it with something that
merely sounds good would be worse than honest. But the synthesis pass
surfaced a real candidate that wasn't articulated in 2019: the
crowdsourced, additive question database. Every interview run, every
contributed question, every star makes the shared bank more useful for
the next user, which is a data flywheel, not just a feature. A competitor
can copy the interface in a weekend. They can't copy years of accumulated,
verified, community-rated question data and the network of contributors
who made it. That's the defensible layer, and it only becomes real once
there's actual usage, so it stays a stated intention rather than a
present-tense claim until the flywheel is actually turning.

**Customer Segments**
Primary: Olivia (Overworked People Ops Manager). Secondary: Andy
(Ambitious External Recruiter). Watching: Ian (Involved Functional
Hiring Manager), Rachel (Recent HR Grad). Full detail in the discovery
and persona master.

**Key Metrics**
*Stale, from the 2019 to 2020 canvas, not current targets:* $3.1 million
revenue in 3 years, 35,000 paid users, $259K MRR (monthly recurring
revenue), $7.38 ARPU (average revenue per user), $265 LTV (lifetime
value), a viral coefficient of 1.7.

**[DECISION NEEDED]** Whether to run a fresh version of this exercise
now with 2026 assumptions, or wait until there's a working product and
at least a handful of real users to anchor real numbers to instead of a
second round of guessing. Leaning toward the second, a guess made today
isn't meaningfully better calibrated than the 2019 guess was, it would
just be a newer guess.

**Channels**
2019 to 2020 channels (Slack and HR groups, LinkedIn invites, content
marketing, cold outreach) are all still directionally plausible. Not
revalidated for 2026. Worth a real look once positioning is locked, this
document isn't the place to guess at 2026 channel mix from scratch.

**Cost Structure**
Not rebuilt here. Genuinely depends on the build decisions that haven't
been made yet (model routing, hosting, whether Argo shares any
infrastructure with Recruiter OS). Premature to price this out before
the PRD exists.

**Revenue Streams**
2019 to 2020 model was freemium, bottom-up SaaS, roughly 90% free to 10%
paid, tiered starting at $4.99, an enterprise tier, and hourly interview
training as an upsell. Flagged in the context seed as unrevalidated
against 2026 buyer expectations. Not rebuilt here for the same reason as
Key Metrics above.

## 6. The Verified Question Bank Story

This is the narrative spine for the "verified, not just fluent" value
proposition, and it was already drafted, in spirit, in Mo's 2019 to 2020
notes. The old marketing language described filtering tens of thousands of
questions down to a top-performing set across industries, roles,
functions, and job levels, refined through expert review over years.

Two things about that. First, the specific numbers in the old notes (a
precise count filtered down to a precise smaller count) were aspirational,
not real, and cannot be used as literal claims until they're literally
true. Stating a fabricated precision would be exactly the kind of thing
that reads as fluent and is actually false, which is the precise failure
Argo exists to protect its users from. The story has to be earned before
it's told at that level of specificity.

Second, and more important: the shape of the story is right, and it's now
buildable for real. A retrieval system (RAG, retrieval-augmented
generation) pulling from a genuinely reviewed bank is what lets Argo
honestly say its questions came from somewhere real, screened by people
who know what a good interview question looks like. That's the whole
difference between Argo and an open tab to a chatbot, and it's the thing
Olivia's sixth job to be done (from the discovery and persona master) is
actually asking for. The narrative and the architecture are the same
decision viewed from two angles.
