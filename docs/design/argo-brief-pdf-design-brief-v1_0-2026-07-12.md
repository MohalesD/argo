# Argo Candidate Brief PDF — An Evidence-Based Design Brief

*Prepared for a Claude planning session and @react-pdf/renderer implementation. Every substantive recommendation is sourced. Where evidence is thin or contested, it is flagged plainly.*

## TL;DR
- **Lead with a one-page, scannable evidence summary — not decoration.** Debrief readers scan documents in the "layer-cake" and "F" patterns (headings and the first words of lines) and "read very little from a wall of text" (Nielsen Norman Group); a résumé's analogous first pass is ~7.4 seconds (Ladders 2018 eye-tracking study). The brief must front-load role context and a set of front-loaded thematic claims, with the verbatim appendix demoted to the back but one jump away. The single highest-ROI move is **not** adding cards — it is fixing the line-height/measure and building a strong heading hierarchy separated by whitespace.
- **Credibility comes from verifiable specificity, not visual polish.** Argo's "evidence not opinion" value proposition is signaled by tight claim→citation→verbatim-quote coupling, named attribution (who said what, to which question), and the deliberate *absence* of scores/rankings — reinforced by an explicit method statement on the page. In a controlled study, adding references moved reader-rated evidence verifiability from 1.30 to 3.86 on a 5-point scale (Cohen's *d* = 1.98), "transform[ing] recommendations from unverifiable assertions into traceable evidence-based information."
- **On paper, prefer whitespace and hairline rules over boxes/cards.** Screen conventions (cards, containers, heavy fills) become noise in print. Butterick's *Practical Typography* is explicit: rules and borders "are best used sparingly… You can usually get equally good results by increasing the space above and below the text. Try that first." Redesign around a ~60–72-character measure, ~1.4 unitless body leading, and a clear type scale — this fixes the current "visually plain / line-height problem" without importing the web view's card system.

## Key Findings

**1. How debrief readers actually read.** Nielsen Norman Group eye-tracking identifies four text-scanning patterns; for a headed, structured document readers use the **layer-cake pattern** (reading headings/subheads, skipping the body beneath) and the **F-pattern** (fixating the top band and the left edge of the content, then trailing off). NN/g's blunt conclusion: users "read very little from a wall of text." Consequences for Argo: headings must carry meaning standing alone; the first 3–5 words of every claim line must front-load the substance; anything important buried mid-paragraph will be missed. The analogous evaluative first pass is fast and layout-driven — the Ladders Inc. 2018 eye-tracking study of 30 recruiters found an average initial screening time of **7.4 seconds** (up from a 6-second figure in 2012), and that the résumés holding attention had "simple layouts with clearly marked section and title headers." Reviewers grant a deeper read only once relevance is signaled, so the brief must earn it in its top third.

**2. What structured-hiring practice already standardizes — and what Argo correctly removes.** The mature convention across Google re:Work, Greenhouse, Lever, and SHRM is a four-part shape: competencies/attributes → **evidence notes** ("what the candidate actually said or did, not a general impression") → rating → overall recommendation. Argo deliberately keeps the first two and drops the last two. Crucially, the evidence-note layer is the part every serious source treats as most defensible: guidance repeatedly insists teams "capture evidence, not just impressions" — write "Clearly explained a complex project by breaking it down into three simple steps," not "good communicator." Greenhouse's own scorecard data object is literally *attributes with typed notes plus custom question/answer pairs* — structurally identical to Argo's thematic claims plus cited responses. In other words, **Argo's document is the evidence layer of a scorecard, stripped of the subjective scoring that creates most legal and bias exposure.**

**3. Structured interviewing is the strongest-validity selection method — the honest anchor for Argo's ethos.** Schmidt & Hunter (1998, *Psychological Bulletin*) placed structured interviews at operational validity **.51** vs **.38** for unstructured. The important correction: Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens (2022, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 107(11), 2040–2068) identified a systematic range-restriction overcorrection in prior meta-analyses and reported lower, more defensible figures: **.42 for structured interviews and .19 for unstructured.** Notably, structured interviews **emerged as the single top-ranked predictor of job performance — above general mental ability (revised down to .31)** — leading the authors to suggest "one might propose structured interviews as the focal predictor against which others are evaluated." (The .48 sometimes cited is a sensitivity-analysis bound, not the headline estimate; .42 is the reported operational validity.) The implication for Argo's framing: the defensible claim is *"structure the capture of what was said,"* not *"predict the hire."* The ethos — **"Argo structures what was said. Humans decide."** — aligns precisely with what the evidence supports and avoids the overclaiming the 2022 reanalysis punished.

**4. What reads as "AI slop" versus "evidence."** Recurring tells of low-effort AI content, per converging practitioner sources: generic/vague statements with no concrete referents; the uniform "bold-preface-then-colon-then-sentence" bullet format; tonal flatness where every claim carries equal weight; hedge-free polished prose; over-reliance on stock transitions ("Moreover," "In conclusion"). The antidote is exactly what Argo owns structurally: **verbatim quotes copied from a real source, specific named questions, and honest variation in what is and isn't known** (including explicit open questions/gaps). The design must foreground those human-provenance signals — quotation marks, the actual question text, "captured note" labeling — because they are the visible proof the content was *retrieved*, not *generated*. A document that admits what it does not know reads as human and careful; one that asserts uniformly confident polish reads as machine output.

**5. Citation and evidence presentation.** Schreieder, Schopf & Färber (2025), "Attribution, Citation, and Quotation: A Survey of Evidence-based Text Generation with LLMs" (arXiv 2508.15396, 134 papers), identify six citation styles; **62% of studies use in-line citations**, which best support *claim-level* verification, while embedded **verbatim quotes** additionally let a reader confirm the exact supporting span. Argo already uses the strongest combination: in-line citation markers on each claim plus a verbatim appendix. Professional-document convention (UN editorial manual, APA, legal appendices/attachments) supports this split — keep the reading surface clean with numeric references; put full verbatim material in a numbered appendix reachable in one jump. The payoff is measurable: in a controlled paired-comparison study (arXiv 2606.06869, N = 25), plans with references scored **4.21 vs 2.95 overall** (Δ = +1.27); **evidence verifiability showed the largest effect (d = 1.98**, rising from 1.30 to 3.86 on a 5-point scale), followed by overall evidence quality (*d* = 1.55) and source credibility (*d* = 1.52), all *p*<sub>adj</sub> < 0.001.

**6. Print typography is not screen typography.** Line length ("measure") should be ~50–75 characters, with **66 the common target** (Bringhurst, echoed by the U.S. Web Design System and UXPin). Body leading ~1.4 in print (screens run higher, 1.5–1.8). The current @react-pdf/renderer "line-height problem" is a known footgun: documented react-pdf issue #2369 shows the `lineHeight` property producing "significant space between lines" when set in absolute units — **line-height must be a unitless multiple (e.g., 1.4)**, with font sizes in pt. Set text left-aligned / ragged-right (justified text renders poorly without good hyphenation). react-pdf also has real layout constraints: flexbox bugs interact with the page `wrap` flag (issue #206), and multi-column flow across page breaks is unreliable — so the brief must be **single-column**.

**7. Boxes vs. rules vs. whitespace on paper.** Butterick's *Practical Typography* ("Rules & borders") is direct: they "are best used sparingly. Ask yourself: do you really need a rule or border to make a visual distinction? You can usually get equally good results by increasing the space above and below the text. Try that first." Rules should be **0.5–1pt**; thicker borders "create noise that upstages the information inside… You want to see the data, not the lines around the data." This directly contradicts porting the web view's cards to paper. On paper, cards/containers add ink, shrink usable measure, fragment the F-pattern scan, and photocopy poorly. **Whitespace + type hierarchy + occasional hairline rules** achieve separation with far less noise.

**8. Legal / compliance framing.** Candidate-evaluation documents are discoverable records. Under EEOC/Title VII recordkeeping (29 CFR 1602), employers must retain hiring records **≥ 1 year (2 years for federal contractors)**, and courts have imposed adverse-inference sanctions for failure to retain (*Austrum v. Federal Cleaning Contractors*, S.D. Fla. 2016). This document *will* be read by lawyers if a claim arises. Separately, the EU AI Act classifies recruitment and candidate-evaluation AI as **high-risk (Annex III §4)**, triggering transparency, human-oversight, and documentation duties; the original deadline was **2 August 2026**, with the Digital Omnibus proposing deferral of Annex III obligations to **2 December 2027** (as of mid-2026, approved by the European Parliament and awaiting final Council adoption — verify current status with counsel). Argo's choices — no scores, no rankings, no hire/no-hire recommendation, no inference of protected characteristics, cite-everything — constitute a strong risk posture: they position Argo as a **documentation/structuring tool that supports human decisions**, not an automated evaluator. But the PDF should **state this explicitly** so a downstream reader (exec, lawyer, regulator) sees the stance on the page rather than having to infer it.

## Details

### Recommended information hierarchy (top to bottom)

1. **Header band — identity + provenance.** Candidate name, role/req, interview date, interviewer name(s), stage. Plus a one-line provenance statement, e.g.: *"This brief contains only claims drawn from interviewer-captured notes. Every claim cites a specific response. Argo does not score, rank, or recommend."* This is the **BLUF of trust** — it tells every downstream reader what the document is and isn't before they read a word. Factual, not marketing.
2. **Role context** (short). What the interview was scoped to assess — the frame the layer-cake scanner needs to interpret everything below.
3. **Thematic sections with cited claims** (the core). Each theme (e.g., "Technical Execution") is a *meaningful* heading; beneath it, claims as short, front-loaded statements each ending in an inline citation marker. This is the primary scan surface — optimize it hardest.
4. **Starred moments.** A small set of highlighted key moments. Demote visually below themes but keep each individually scannable (these are the "spotted pattern" targets).
5. **Open questions for the team.** Gaps/follow-ups. **Keep this prominent, not buried** — it is a major anti-slop *and* pro-fairness signal (the document admits what it does not know) and the most actionable content for a panel deciding next steps.
6. **Cited responses appendix.** Numbered, verbatim question + captured note per citation. Demoted to the back but reachable in one jump. This is the verification layer; it should read like a reference apparatus (differentiated type, quotation formatting), not more prose.

**What to cut/demote:** any restatement, any adjective not tied to a citation, any summary that isn't traceable. **If a line can't cite, it shouldn't be a claim.**

### Concrete typography & layout spec (print/PDF)
- **Page:** A4 or US Letter, single column, margins ≥ 0.75–1 in to hold the measure down.
- **Measure:** 60–72 characters per line for body — this may make the text column narrower than the full page width, which is correct for print.
- **Body:** ~10–11 pt serif or humanist sans; line-height as a **unitless ~1.35–1.45** (never an absolute pt value — this is the fix for the current line-height bug).
- **Type scale (4 clear steps):** section headings ~15–16 pt bold; theme subheads ~12–13 pt; body ~10.5 pt; citation/appendix ~9–9.5 pt. Headings take *less* relative leading (1.1–1.2).
- **Separation:** primarily whitespace between sections; add a single **0.5–0.75 pt hairline rule under section headings only** if space alone reads weak. No filled cards, no rounded containers, no drop shadows.
- **Claims:** each claim a short paragraph or hung item; inline citation marker (superscript number or bracketed `[n]`) at claim end. Avoid the bold-preface-colon bullet cliché that reads as AI.
- **Quotes/verbatim (appendix):** present the question in one style (e.g., medium-weight, labeled "Q") and the captured note in a differentiated block (left-indent with a hairline **left rule**, or roman in quotation marks) so question and response are instantly distinguishable. A thin left rule on a quote block is the one place a rule clearly earns its keep.
- **Color:** minimal; one restrained accent for citation markers/links so verification targets are findable in the F-pattern scan. Confirm it survives grayscale printing.
- **Running elements:** repeat candidate/role and a page number in a footer (react-pdf `fixed`), plus a short confidentiality line.

### Presenting cited evidence for fast verification
- Keep the inline marker **adjacent to the claim it supports** (claim-level attribution), never floated to a distant margin.
- Make citation numbers monotonic and identical between body and appendix; if the renderer supports it, make them internal links.
- In the appendix, show the **exact question text verbatim** and the **interviewer's captured note verbatim**, visually separated. This is Argo's proof-of-retrieval; treat it as a first-class surface, not fine print.
- **Never paraphrase inside quotes.** Verbatim is the entire credibility mechanism.

### Visual devices — use vs. avoid on paper
- **Use:** whitespace; a strong type scale; hairline rules under headings; a single left-rule quote block for verbatim notes; restrained accent color for citations; consistent left/ragged-right alignment.
- **Avoid:** cards/containers with fills or borders; drop shadows; heavy dividers; multi-column layouts; icon noise; **badges/pills/progress bars or any rating-like graphic** (these would visually *imply* the scoring Argo deliberately doesn't do); emoji; dense color blocks that don't photocopy.

### Disclaimers / compliance-driven content
- **Provenance statement** in the header (above), plus a fuller **method note** near the appendix: how claims are produced (retrieval + citation from captured notes), and the explicit statement that Argo produces **no scores, rankings, or hire/no-hire recommendations and does not infer protected characteristics**.
- **Human-decision framing:** restate **"Argo structures what was said. Humans decide."** somewhere durable — both brand ethos and an EU AI Act–aligned posture (decision-support with human oversight, not automated decision-making).
- **Confidentiality + records notice:** a short footer line noting the document is a confidential hiring record; given EEOC retention rules and discoverability, this cues downstream readers to handle it as an evidentiary record.
- **No demographic/protected-characteristic content** anywhere — and state that this is by design.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Fix the fundamentals (highest ROI; do first).** Set the body measure to ~60–72 chars, line-height to a unitless ~1.4, establish a 4-step type scale, and replace weak section breaks with whitespace + optional hairline heading rules. This alone resolves the stated "visually plain / weak separation / line-height" problems with **no card system**. *Benchmark:* a reader can scan headings + the first line of each claim and understand the candidate in under 60 seconds.

**Stage 2 — Make evidence the visual hero.** Rebuild the citation marker + appendix as a tight, linked, verbatim apparatus (question vs. note clearly differentiated; one left-rule quote block). Add the header provenance statement and the method note. *Benchmark:* any claim can be traced to its verbatim source in one jump, and a reader who has never used Argo can articulate "this is evidence, not an AI opinion" unprompted.

**Stage 3 — Harden the framing.** Add the confidentiality/records footer, the "structures what was said; humans decide" line, and the explicit no-scores/no-inference statement. *Benchmark:* a lawyer or exec reading cold understands the document's evidentiary stance and limits.

**Guardrails that would change these recommendations:**
- If user testing shows readers want **side-by-side candidate comparison**, resist adding scores; instead consider comparing *themes* and *open questions* across candidates.
- If the PDF must reliably print in **grayscale**, drop accent color entirely and rely on weight and rules.
- If react-pdf's layout limits block **linked citations**, fall back to stable numeric cross-references rather than fighting the renderer.

## Caveats
- **The validity literature is contested; I've reported the corrected figures deliberately.** The .51 structured-interview number is still widely quoted but was revised to **.42** by Sackett et al. (2022); unstructured to **.19**; GMA down to **.31**. The takeaway for Argo (structure the capture, don't predict) is robust either way — but do not let marketing copy revert to the inflated numbers, as that is exactly the overclaiming the reanalysis corrected.
- **Design specifics are evidence-informed ranges, not laws.** The 66-character measure and ~1.4 leading are well-supported conventions (Bringhurst, USWDS, Butterick), but final values should be tuned to the chosen typeface and tested on real printed output.
- **AI-slop "tells" are practitioner consensus, not peer-reviewed findings.** The specific formatting cues come from content-practitioner and detection-tool sources; treat them as credible heuristics.
- **The verifiability/credibility effect sizes come from adjacent domains.** The *d* = 1.98 verifiability finding is from an LLM medical-plan study (N = 25) and the 62% in-line-citation figure from an LLM text-generation survey; the *direction* (references sharply increase perceived verifiability and credibility) transfers well to a hiring brief, but the exact magnitudes are not from hiring research.
- **EU AI Act timing is in flux.** The Annex III deferral to Dec 2027 was, as of mid-2026, approved by Parliament and awaiting final Council adoption — verify current status with counsel before relying on any date. Argo's fundamental posture (no scoring/ranking, human decides) is the durable mitigation regardless of date.
- **react-pdf capabilities should be spike-tested.** Internal links, `fixed` footers, and hairline rules generally work, but confirm in the version in use before committing the design to them; the library has documented flexbox-with-`wrap` and `lineHeight`-unit bugs.
